Shader arithmetic: integers vs floats — what's actually faster?

Every pixel your GPU draws is decided by a tiny program called a shader. When that program does colour math, it can use integers or floating point — and it turns out the choice matters more than you'd think. This page summarises a real measurement of both, on a real browser GPU.

The question

Every pixel a WebGL game draws is the output of a tiny program running on your graphics card — a shader. When that program has to multiply two colour values together, it can do the arithmetic in integers or in floating point. Integer arithmetic is exact but limited in range; floating point is smooth but can lose precision. Which is faster? It depends entirely on your GPU and its driver — and it's not always what you'd guess.

To find out, we timed a realistic shader workload — the per-pixel colour pipeline of a real WebGL game, including its texture tint, scanline dim, and fade effects — written two ways: all-integer and all-float. Then we also timed the precision settings and a couple of other small choices that shader authors make. The answers might surprise you.

Read together, they give three practical rules for writing WebGL shaders:

  • Prefer integers for colour math when you can. An all-integer colour pipeline was ~1.6× faster than the all-float version on this GPU. Not every GPU will agree — but integers are at worst tied, and they're exact.
  • The default precision is the fast one. Both "mediump" runs beat "highp" by ~1.2×. You don't gain speed by asking for more precision — only compatibility problems (highp in the pixel stage isn't even always allowed).
  • Power-of-two divisors win. Dividing by 128 (which the hardware can turn into a shift) beat dividing by 127 (a "real" division) by 1.13×.

But there's a catch: you can't actually write ×0.9 in integers (see below) — so the practical shader is a mix of integer and floating-point. The last row measured that mix against a hypothetical all-integer rewrite and found no difference — the parts that were already integer were the expensive ones, and the leftover floating-point parts were cheap. So: go as integer as you comfortably can, and don't sweat the rest.

The catch: you can't write ×0.9 in integers

Integers win the arithmetic — but they're awkward to write. A game effect that dims a pixel by 10% is a multiplication by 0.9 — trivial in floating point, impossible in integers. The game expresses it as r * 90 / 100: multiply by 90, then divide by 100. That division by a non-power-of-two is exactly the kind of op the benchmark shows is slower than a power-of-two (/128 beat /127 by 1.13×).

ES 1.00 makes this worse: it has no bit-shift operators at all (>>, << and friends are WebGL 2 / GLSL ES 3.00 only), so you can't even cheat with the "shifts instead of divides" trick. The practical options are r * N / 256 (multiply by a small constant, then divide by a power of two — which the hardware can turn into a shift) or simply leave the fractional effects in floating point.

And that's exactly what happens in practice: the game's shader keeps the fractional dimming (the scanline, the vignette) in floating point, and the benchmark's final row shows that costs nothing (1.003×). The integer pipeline wins the arithmetic; the awkward fractions are simply not worth forcing into integers.

Why this matters

  • Shaders run billions of times a second. A 1.6× difference in a multiply is a real frame-rate difference in a game that draws every pixel every frame.
  • The answer is not universal. The same shaders can rank differently on an AMD card, a phone's Mali GPU, or Apple's Metal translator. The numbers above are true for this one GPU — your GPU may differ. The rules (prefer integers, prefer the default precision, prefer power-of-two divisors) are safe bets, not guarantees.
  • Precision and speed go together here. The exact option (integers) and the fast option were the same one — which is the nice outcome, but not one you should count on without measuring.
